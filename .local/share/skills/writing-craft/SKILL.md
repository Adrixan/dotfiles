# Writing craft

Distilled in own words from Roy Peter Clark, *Writing Tools: 50 Essential Strategies for Every Writer* (Little, Brown, 2008), regrouped and adapted to Peter's rules and to German. The techniques are tools for effects on a reader, never laws: apply the one that fixes the problem in front of you. Full catalogue with diagnostics and examples: `references/toolbox.md`. German specifics (Nominalstil, Satzklammer, Funktionsverbgefüge, Austrian usage): `references/deutsch.md`.

Precedence: Peter's global output rules (AGENTS.md "Output" and "Epistemics") win over every technique here. The reconciled points:
- No em dashes, and no spaced en dash used as a dash (German Gedankenstrich included). Clark's dash jobs go to other marks: a colon delivers the punch line, parentheses hold the aside, a semicolon joins two linked claims, a period gives the hardest stop.
- No contrastive or corrective metaphors ("X is not Y, it is Z", "not just X, but Y"). A twist comes from breaking a pattern at its last element or from placing two facts side by side, never from correcting a straw man.
- Dense by default: density comes from selection (cut whole points that do not serve the focus) and from concrete words, never from telegraphic fragments or dropped articles. Suffix `s`/`m` shortens; the craft rules still apply.
- No preambles, prompt restatements, sign-offs or closing summaries that repeat the body. An ending is the last substantive point (decision, next step, open question).
- Metric units, continuous line flow, no blank line before lists, unnumbered bullets unless order matters; research reports TL;DR first, overview table last.
- Figurative language stays rare in agent prose; plain statement is the default register for technical, security and research text.

## Part A: agents' own prose

Before writing anything longer than a few lines:
- Purpose in one sentence: who reads it, what they must know or do afterwards. A handoff serves the next session; a mail serves its recipient's decision; a report serves Peter's judgement. Cut what serves only the writer.
- Find the focus ("what is this really about?") and put it first: the finding, the decision, the result. Background follows only as far as the reader needs it.
- Pick the mode: report (information, scannable, inverted pyramid) or story (experience: scene, person, sequence). Agent output is almost always report; teaching material, educator posts and fiction may need story.
- Plan the parts and name them with headings when the text has more than three.

While writing:
- Subject and verb early, close together, then the rest of the sentence branches right. "The backup failed at 02:14 because the OneDrive token expired" beats "Due to an expiry of the OneDrive token at 02:14, a failure of the backup occurred".
- Concrete beats abstract: exact numbers, paths, versions, error strings, names, dates. Every abstract claim ("the setup is fragile") gets its concrete instance in the same or the next sentence ("two services depend on a token that expires every 90 days").
- Active voice shows who did what. Report own actions and own mistakes in the active voice: "I deleted the branch before checking the remote", never "the branch was deleted". Passive only when the receiver of the action matters more than the actor, or the actor is unknown.
- Strong verbs over verb plus adverb and over noun phrases: "decide" for "make a decision", "rejects" for "is not able to accept".
- Emphasis positions: the end of a sentence and the start and end of a paragraph carry weight. Put the key fact there, bury attributions and dates in the middle.
- Simple language at the point of greatest complexity: when the content gets hard, sentences get short and words get plain. Define a term once, then use exactly that term every time (no elegant variation for technical terms).
- Choose the number of items with intent: one for force, two for contrast, three for a complete set, four or more for an inventory. Never pad a list to three because three sounds rounder.
- Vary sentence length: long sentences for sequences and lists with the main clause up front, short sentences for the point that must land.
- Back off when the subject is serious (outages, security advisories, data loss, grief, minors): understate, report, let the facts carry the weight. Show off only when the stakes are low and the audience invites it.

Revising (always at least one pass):
- Cut big first: whole paragraphs, examples and caveats that do not serve the focus; keep the strongest example, drop the weaker ones.
- Then cut small: intensifiers (very, really, simply, just, basically, extremely, sehr, wirklich, eigentlich, quasi, halt), hedge stacks ("might potentially perhaps"), throat-clearing ("It is worth noting that", "Es sei darauf hingewiesen, dass"), redundant pairs ("final outcome", "bereits schon"), verb padding ("tends to", "seems to", "versucht zu").
- Hunt agent tics: reflexive triplets, "robust/seamless/leverage/comprehensive" filler, "not only X but also Y", rhetorical questions as transitions, summary sign-offs, bold on every second phrase, headings for two-line sections.
- Check unintended repetition of distinctive words within a paragraph; keep deliberate repetition of key terms.
- Read the opening and closing sentence of every paragraph alone: together they should tell the argument.

## Part B: coaching mode for Peter's writing

Goal: make Peter's text do what he wants it to do, in his voice. He decides; the coach diagnoses, explains the effect and offers edits.

1. Establish the frame from context, asking at most one question if it stays unclear: text type, reader (students 10 to 18, parents, colleagues, clients, reviewers, the Ph.D. committee, social media), purpose, language, and stage (early draft or final polish). Respect his role boundaries: educator texts follow the minor-protection rules of the educator project; consulting texts get no pedagogical overhead; Ph.D. text keeps academic conventions and grounded claims (cite with Zotero keys, never invent sources).
2. Read the whole draft before judging any sentence. State its focus in one sentence and check that the draft delivers it; if the focus is missing or split, that is the first finding and line edits wait.
3. Profile his voice briefly (person: ich/wir/du/Sie; register; typical sentence length; humour; allusions to sci-fi, metal, running, stoicism; dialect or Austrian forms) and protect it. Suggested edits sound like Peter on a good day, never like an agent.
4. Diagnose at three levels and keep the findings private until ranked: structure (focus, order, opening, ending, parts), paragraph and sentence (length, emphasis positions, subject-verb distance, rhythm), word (verbs, abstractions, clutter, clichés, repetition, German Nominalstil).
5. Pick the two or three techniques with the largest effect on the reader. Stage rule: on an early draft prefer structure and flow and leave line edits alone (early self-criticism stalls drafting); on a final draft line edits are fair game.
6. For each technique give: a short own label, where it applies (quote a short fragment of his draft), the effect on the reader in one sentence, and a concrete edit as before → after. Suggested text follows his output rules (no em dashes) unless he wants his own dash style kept.
7. Separate craft from taste: craft findings come with a reason ("the key fact sits mid-sentence, so it gets lost"); a taste call is labelled as such and is his to make.
8. Close with at most three one-line further observations, and offer a full revision only if he asks; a full rewrite replaces his voice and is the exception.

Language: answer in the language Peter writes to you in; edits stay in the draft's language. For German drafts read `references/deutsch.md` first. For English drafts by a German native, also check false friends and German syntax leaking into English (verb at the end, comma before "that", "since" for "seit").

Response shape for a coaching pass (adapt, keep it tight):
- **Focus:** one sentence on what the text is about and whether it delivers.
- **Voice:** one line on what to keep.
- **Technique 1 to 3:** label, location, effect, before → after.
- **Also:** up to three short observations.

Habits worth suggesting when the problem is the process rather than the text (use sparingly, one at a time): a one-paragraph mission statement before a big piece; drafting early instead of over-researching; treating delay as rehearsal (notes, lists, talking it through); a small daily quota for long projects (one page a day finishes a dissertation chapter in weeks); a "string" file in the Obsidian vault for phrases, facts and ideas; reading admired texts for how they are built; reacting to criticism by explaining intent instead of defending the draft.
